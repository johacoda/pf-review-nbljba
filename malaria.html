Malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Why this disease is important

Nigeria has more malaria deaths than any other country in Africa: nearly a third of the region's total.

Children under five account for about three in four malaria deaths in Africa, and pregnancy raises the risk of severe disease.

Years of repeated malaria can cause tropical splenomegaly syndrome: a large spleen, a high serum IgM and few parasites in the blood.

Consider this case

A 26-year-old man, a farmer, presents with a dragging discomfort in the left upper abdomen for a year and increasing tiredness. Since childhood he's had fevers several times a year, each treated at home with drugs bought from a chemist.

On examination his conjunctivae are pale. The spleen is firm, non-tender and reaches 18 cm below the left costal margin. The liver is just palpable.

Investigations: haemoglobin 7.1 g/dL, white cell count 2.8 × 10⁹/L with a relative lymphocytosis, platelets 82 × 10⁹/L. A thin film shows scanty P. falciparum rings. Serum IgM is far above the local reference range, and malarial antibody titres are high. Serum protein electrophoresis shows a broad, polyclonal rise in the gamma globulins. He starts long-term proguanil, and six months later the spleen is smaller.

Why is his spleen so large, and why is it not dark with malarial pigment?

Definition of malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Malaria is infection of red cells by Plasmodium, carried by the bite of the female Anopheles mosquito. In falciparum malaria, , of infected cells in small vessels and released from ruptured cells shape what you see in the organs. Tropical splenomegaly syndrome, now called hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly, is massive from an abnormal immune response to repeated malaria, often with .

Quick checkWhy is tropical splenomegaly syndrome now called hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly?

Because the immune response, not the parasite, enlarges the spleen. Years of malarial antigen drive B cells to make far too much IgM, and the spleen grows as it clears the immune complexes. Parasites are usually scanty, and liver and spleen biopsies show no malarial pigment.

Gross morphology of malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Key facts

  • In acute malaria the spleen is enlarged and darkened brown by haemozoin in its macrophages.
  • In tropical splenomegaly syndrome the spleen is massive, and biopsies show no malarial pigment.
  • In cerebral malaria the brain is swollen, and in many fatal cases haemorrhages stud the white matter.

In malaria the gross changes come from pigment, from blocked small vessels and from a spleen that has worked for years. The spleen looks different in acute malaria and in tropical splenomegaly syndrome.

Histology of malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Key facts

  • In cerebral malaria, the brain's capillaries are packed with red cells carrying P. falciparum.
  • In acute malaria, Kupffer cells and splenic macrophages fill with brown-black haemozoin.
  • In tropical splenomegaly syndrome, the liver shows sinusoidal lymphocytosis and no malarial pigment.

Down the microscope, acute malaria shows parasites and pigment, and tropical splenomegaly syndrome shows lymphocytes with no pigment. Look for packed capillaries in the brain and pigment-laden macrophages in the liver and spleen.

Differential diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Key facts

  • A massive spleen here may be malaria, tropical splenomegaly syndrome, chronic myeloid leukaemia, myelofibrosis, lymphoma or schistosomiasis.
  • Chronic myeloid leukaemia raises the white count with neutrophils and immature granulocytes, and carries the BCR-ABL fusion gene.

A massive spleen in a Nigerian adult has a short list of causes, and tropical splenomegaly syndrome is one. Each of the others leaves a mark in the blood or the tissue that tropical splenomegaly syndrome doesn't.

Pathogenesis of malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Key facts

  • Fever in malaria comes with each release of merozoites from rupturing red cells.
  • In falciparum malaria, PfEMP1 on infected red cells binds endothelium, so the cells stick in capillaries.
  • In tropical splenomegaly syndrome, too much IgM forms immune complexes, and the spleen enlarges as it clears them.

Acute malaria harms the body in two ways: infected red cells rupture, and falciparum-infected cells block small vessels. Tropical splenomegaly syndrome comes later, from the immune response to years of infection.

Pathogenesis of falciparum malaria, step by step

Step 1 of 6

Sporozoites reach the liver

The female Anopheles injects sporozoites as she feeds. They travel in the blood to the liver, enter hepatocytes and develop into merozoites. After one to four weeks the hepatocytes rupture and release them. The patient isn't ill yet: only the red cell stage causes disease.

Merozoites invade red cells

A merozoite enters a red cell inside a vacuole. Inside, it grows into a ring-form trophozoite, which feeds on haemoglobin and leaves a brown pigment, , then divides into a schizont.

Red cells rupture, and fever follows

The schizont splits into merozoites, which burst the red cell and invade new ones. Each release of merozoites brings shaking chills and fever. Ruptured cells free haemozoin, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.

Falciparum cells stick to vessel walls

In P. falciparum, maturing parasites put PfEMP1 into knobs on the red cell surface. PfEMP1 binds ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and CD36 on endothelium, so the infected cells stop in capillary beds. This takes the older parasites out of the circulating blood and packs them into small vessels.

Blocked vessels in the brain and placenta

In cerebral malaria the brain's small vessels become engorged and blocked with infected cells. In pregnancy, falciparum parasites sequester in the intervillous spaces of the placenta.

Red cells are lost

Macrophages destroy parasitised red cells outside the vessels, and some cells lyse inside them. In chronic malaria, an anaemia of chronic disorders and red cells pooled in an enlarged spleen add to the loss.

Clinical features of malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Key facts

  • Uncomplicated malaria causes fever, chills and headache, often milder in people who've had malaria before.
  • In children, severe malaria presents mainly as coma, metabolic acidosis or severe anaemia.
  • Tropical splenomegaly syndrome presents with a massive spleen, severe anaemia and repeated infections.

Uncomplicated malaria is a fever with chills and headache. Severe malaria adds features you can group by their cause: blocked vessels and lost red cells.

Course of malaria and tropical splenomegaly syndrome

The course of malaria depends on the immunity the patient has built. Tropical splenomegaly syndrome regresses when malarial antigen is removed, and a spleen that doesn't regress needs another explanation.

A 35-year-old woman was diagnosed with tropical splenomegaly syndrome a year ago. She has taken long-term antimalarial prophylaxis since, but her spleen is no smaller and her lymphocyte count is rising.

What do you think of first?

CorrectThe right concern. Tropical splenomegaly syndrome should regress once malarial antigen is removed. A spleen that doesn't, with rising lymphocytes, raises a lymphoma. Not this oneThe spleen in tropical splenomegaly syndrome is enlarged by lymphoid proliferation, not fibrosis. It shouldn't stay the same size once the antigen is gone.

Key facts

Malaria in pregnancy

Key facts

  • In placental malaria, P. falciparum sequesters in the intervillous spaces by binding chondroitin sulfate A.
  • Placental malaria causes severe maternal anaemia, low birth weight, premature delivery and fetal loss.
  • A woman is most at risk of placental malaria in her first pregnancy, and gains immunity over later ones.

In pregnancy the placenta becomes a site where falciparum parasites sequester. A woman who has coped with malaria for years can fall ill in her first pregnancy, and the baby pays for it too.

Ward

A 19-year-old woman in her first pregnancy grew up in a rural community and rarely falls ill with malaria. She delivers at term a baby of low birth weight. She's pale, and her haemoglobin is low. The placenta is sent to the laboratory.

Where in the placenta would you look for the parasites?

In the intervillous spaces, the maternal blood spaces of the placenta. Falciparum-infected red cells sequester there.

Why has malaria caught up with her now, after years of coping with it?

The parasites that sequester in the placenta bind it differently from those that infected her before. In a first pregnancy she has no antibodies against them. She gains that immunity over later pregnancies.

What has placental malaria cost her and her baby?

Severe anaemia for her, and low birth weight for her baby. Placental malaria can also cause premature delivery and fetal loss.

Life cycle of Plasmodium

Key facts

  • Sporozoites from the mosquito infect the liver first; only the red cell stage causes illness.
  • In P. vivax, P. ovale and P. malariae, infected red cells burst together, so fever comes in regular waves.
  • P. vivax and P. ovale leave hypnozoites in the liver, so they can relapse weeks or years later.

Plasmodium lives part of its life in people and part in the mosquito. Each stage of the life cycle explains a feature of the disease or the film.

Connect this to other specialties
Drugs that reach the liver stage

Plasmodium is a single-celled parasite (a protozoan) that causes malaria. Four species typically infect people: P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale and P. malariae. The female Anopheles mosquito carries them, and humans are their only natural reservoir.

The parasite changes form at each stage, and each form has its own name.

Life cycle of Plasmodium, step by step

  1. An infected female Anopheles mosquito bites and injects from its salivary glands. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver and quickly invade liver cells.

  2. Inside the liver cell each sporozoite grows into a with thousands of nuclei. After one to four weeks the liver cell bursts and releases into the blood. The liver stage causes no symptoms.

  3. Each merozoite enters a red cell and becomes a ring-shaped . The trophozoite grows and divides into a schizont, and the red cell bursts, releasing new merozoites that invade more red cells. Only this blood stage causes illness.

  4. Infected red cells burst together, so chills and fever come in waves: every 48 hours in P. vivax and P. ovale, and every 72 hours in P. malariae. P. falciparum also has a cycle of about 48 hours, but its red cells burst less in step, so the fever is often irregular or continuous.

  5. Some parasites in red cells become male and female instead of dividing. A mosquito takes them up when it bites. They fuse in its gut, and the new forms grow into sporozoites that move to its salivary glands.

Remember. P. vivax and P. ovale leave dormant in the liver. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse, even after the blood is cleared. P. falciparum and P. malariae have a single liver cycle, which ends within four weeks.

Identifying Plasmodium species on a blood film

Pathogenesis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome

Step 1 of 6

Repeated malaria

Tropical splenomegaly syndrome is found in malarious zones of the tropics, Nigeria among them, in people infected again and again over years. Malaria is probably the root cause.

Antigen reaches the spleen

The spleen filters malarial antigen out of the blood. Macrophages and dendritic cells in its marginal zone take the antigen up and present it to B and T cells, which starts an adaptive immune response.

An abnormal response

Continual malarial antigen drives an abnormal host response. Lymphocytes proliferate, mainly in the liver and spleen, and the proliferation is reactive and relatively benign. Liver and spleen biopsies show no malarial pigment, because this isn't active infection.

IgM rises

Serum IgM rises high, and malarial antibody titres are high. IgM is the immunoglobulin mainly raised in blood protozoan infections such as malaria. Some patients also develop a marked lymphocytosis in the blood.

Immune complexes form

The excess IgM forms large immune complexes, which persist in the macrophages of the spleen and liver. The spleen enlarges as it clears them, and the higher the IgM, the bigger the spleen.

The spleen grows massive

The spleen becomes massive and the liver enlarges too; portal hypertension may follow. A spleen this size pools red cells, white cells and platelets, so the anaemia is often severe and leucopenia is usual.

Key facts

  • Parasitised red cells stick in small vessels and block them, so tissues switch to anaerobic glycolysis.
  • Lactic acidosis is a high anion gap metabolic acidosis: bicarbonate falls and lactate takes its place.

Pathogenesis of lactic acidosis in falciparum malaria, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Infected red cells stick

As P. falciparum matures inside the red cell, a parasite protein, PfEMP1, on the cell surface binds receptors on endothelium, so infected cells stick in small vessels instead of circulating.

Small vessels blocked

Packed with stuck cells, the small vessels of the brain and other organs carry less blood. How much an organ fails depends on how many of its vessels are blocked.

Anaerobic glycolysis

Cells short of oxygen make energy by anaerobic glycolysis, which ends in lactate. Glucose is used up fast at the same time, so hypoglycaemia often comes with the acidosis.

Bicarbonate used up

Lactic acid releases hydrogen ions, and bicarbonate is used up buffering them. Plasma bicarbonate falls, and lactate takes its place as an anion that isn't measured, so the widens: a high anion gap metabolic acidosis.

Lungs compensate

Acidosis drives rapid, deep, laboured breathing, which blows off carbon dioxide and limits the fall in pH. In children with malaria this breathing is often called respiratory distress, but the acid comes from the tissues, not the lungs.

Antimalarial drugs and the malaria life cycle

Key facts

  • Only the blood stage of Plasmodium causes illness, so every antimalarial that treats malaria kills it.
  • Primaquine and tafenoquine kill the dormant liver forms of P. vivax and P. ovale; blood-stage drugs don't.
  • Artemisinins kill young gametocytes but not mature ones; primaquine makes falciparum gametocytes non-infective.

Each antimalarial drug acts on particular stages of the parasite's life cycle, and that decides what it's used for.

Antimalarial drugs are classified by the stage of Plasmodium they kill. kill parasites inside red cells, kill forms in the liver, and kill the sexual forms that infect the mosquito.

  3. Schizonts grow in liver cells
  5. Hypnozoites lie dormant (vivax, ovale)
  7. Merozoites invade red cells
  9. Blood-stage cycle causes illness
  11. Gametocytes form in red cells
  13. Mosquito takes up gametocytes

Antimalarial drugs at each stage of the malaria life cycle

Step 1 of 5

Liver stage

Sporozoites from the mosquito invade liver cells and grow into tissue schizonts. Primaquine and tafenoquine act on these liver stages, and proguanil has some activity there. The artemisinins have no effect on liver stages, quinine isn't active against them, and chloroquine isn't reliably active.

Dormant liver forms

In P. vivax and P. ovale some liver forms, the , lie dormant and cause relapses after the blood has been cleared. Most drugs don't eradicate them, but primaquine and tafenoquine do. A blood-stage drug plus one of them is a .

Blood stage

Merozoites from the liver invade red cells and multiply, and only this stage causes illness. Every drug that treats malaria kills it. The artemisinins act very rapidly; lumefantrine, amodiaquine, chloroquine and quinine act here too. Pyrimethamine and proguanil act slowly.

Gametocytes

Some blood-stage parasites become , the forms a mosquito takes up. Artemisinins kill young gametocytes but not mature ones. Primaquine makes falciparum gametocytes non-infective, so some areas add it to cut transmission. Chloroquine isn't reliably active against gametocytes.

Prevention

Every drug that prevents malaria kills blood-stage parasites before there are enough to cause illness, and a few, such as primaquine and proguanil, also act on the liver. In Africa pregnant women get sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine after the first trimester, and in the Sahel children get seasonal amodiaquine with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine.

Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)

Key facts

  • Artemisinins clear parasites very fast, but a short course of one alone is often followed by recrudescence.
  • In an ACT the longer-acting partner drug clears the parasites left once the artemisinin has gone.
  • Artemether-lumefantrine and artesunate-amodiaquine are the standard ACTs for uncomplicated falciparum malaria in most of Africa.

An artemisinin clears the parasite very fast but is gone from the blood within hours. It's always paired with a longer-acting partner drug, and the pair is called an artemisinin-based combination therapy, or ACT.

Malarial pigment in the packed vessels probably gives the brain its grey colour.

This spleen came from a 25-year-old man who died of falciparum malaria. It's enlarged and soft, and the cut surface is dark brown. Describe it, and say what causes the colour.

An enlarged spleen, darkened brown on its cut surface. The colour is haemozoin, malarial pigment, taken up by the macrophages of the red pulp.

Where does the pigment come from?

The parasite digests haemoglobin in its food vacuole and crystallises the toxic haem into haemozoin. When the red cell ruptures, the pigment is released and macrophages take it up.

Why is the spleen enlarged?

Host defences drive a marked hyperplasia of mononuclear phagocytes, which enlarges the spleen.

How would the spleen of tropical splenomegaly syndrome differ?

It's massive, and it has no malarial pigment, because tropical splenomegaly syndrome isn't active infection.

A dark brown, slightly enlarged liver. Kupffer cells have multiplied and filled with haemozoin released from ruptured red cells.

The spleen, the lymph nodes and the bone marrow.

That it's active. In tropical splenomegaly syndrome the liver shows a sinusoidal lymphocytosis and no pigment.

Tell me what you know about tropical splenomegaly syndrome.

Massive splenomegaly from an abnormal immune response to repeated malaria, now called hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly. How would you make the diagnosis?

Against Fakunle's criteria. The major criteria are a spleen more than 10 cm below the costal margin with no other cause, a serum IgM more than 2 standard deviations above the local mean, raised malarial antibody titres, and a good response to long-term antimalarial prophylaxis. Now the exception. How would you tell it from splenic marginal zone lymphoma?

Both cause a massive spleen, sometimes with a lymphocytosis. Splenic marginal zone lymphoma puts monoclonal B cells with villous projections in the blood, and its spleen doesn't shrink when malaria is suppressed. Morbid Anatomy

Tell me what you know about tropical splenomegaly syndrome.

Tropical splenomegaly syndrome, now called hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly, is massive enlargement of the spleen from an abnormal immune response to repeated malaria. It isn't active infection: parasites are scanty and biopsies show no malarial pigment. B cells make far too much IgM, which forms immune complexes, and the spleen and liver enlarge from a reactive lymphoproliferation.

  1. How would you make the diagnosis?

    Against Fakunle's criteria. A persistent spleen more than 10 cm below the costal margin with no other cause, a serum IgM more than 2 standard deviations above the local mean, raised malarial antibody titres, and a good response to long-term antimalarial prophylaxis. Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis, hypersplenism and lymphocyte proliferation support it.

  2. How does it differ from splenic marginal zone lymphoma?

    Both cause a massive spleen, and the two are often hard to tell apart clinically. In splenic marginal zone lymphoma, monoclonal B cells with villous projections circulate, and the spleen doesn't shrink when malaria is suppressed.

Tell me about cerebral malaria.

Cerebral malaria is coma in falciparum malaria, caused by sequestration. PfEMP1 on infected red cells binds endothelium, and the cells block the brain's small vessels. At autopsy the capillaries are packed with parasitised red cells, and the brain is swollen and may be slate grey. In many fatal cases petechial and ring haemorrhages stud the white matter.

  1. Why must you check the glucose in a child in coma with malaria?

    Because hypoglycaemia can cause the coma in its own right, and in severe malaria it often comes with lactic acidosis.

  2. Why do adults who grew up with malaria rarely get it?

    Repeated infection has built partial immunity, which holds the parasite count down. Cerebral malaria typically affects people with little or no immunity.

Tell me about the spleen in acute malaria.

In acute malaria the spleen is enlarged and darkened, and its cut surface is dark brown. Ruptured red cells release haemozoin, which the red pulp macrophages take up, and mononuclear phagocytes multiply. The spleen in tropical splenomegaly syndrome can be just as big, but it has no pigment.

Microbiology and Parasitology

Tell me about the life cycle of Plasmodium.

The female Anopheles mosquito injects sporozoites, which infect liver cells. Liver schizonts release merozoites, which invade red cells and multiply. Some become gametocytes, which a mosquito takes up.

  1. Which stage causes the illness?

    The red cell stage. The liver stage causes no symptoms.

  2. Why does vivax malaria relapse when falciparum malaria doesn't?

    P. vivax and P. ovale leave hypnozoites, dormant liver forms that wake weeks or years later. P. falciparum has a single liver cycle that ends within four weeks.

  3. Why does the fever come in waves?

    Infected red cells burst together at the end of each cycle, releasing merozoites: every 48 hours in vivax and ovale, every 72 hours in malariae. In falciparum the fever is often irregular.

Tell me how you'd identify the species of Plasmodium on a blood film.

On a thin Giemsa film, by the size of the infected red cell and the shape of the parasite. P. falciparum shows small rings, sometimes several in one cell, and banana-shaped gametocytes in red cells of normal size.

  1. How do you tell P. ovale from P. falciparum?

    P. ovale infects an oval red cell, often with ragged edges and Schüffner's dots, and holds a compact parasite. P. falciparum shows thin rings in a normal red cell, with no Schüffner's dots.

  2. Why are usually only rings and gametocytes seen in falciparum malaria?

    Older stages carry PfEMP1, which binds capillary walls, so they stay in the tissues.

  3. Why is vivax malaria rare in West Africa?

    P. vivax enters red cells through the Duffy antigen, and most people of West African origin lack it.

Tell me about the laboratory diagnosis of malaria.

Microscopy of thick and thin films, or a rapid diagnostic test. The thick film detects parasites, the thin film names the species and gives the parasitaemia, and the rapid test detects parasite antigen.

  1. A first film is negative. Can you exclude malaria?

    No. Films are repeated every 12 to 24 hours, three sets in all, before malaria is ruled out.

  2. What does a positive HRP2 rapid test mean in a child treated two weeks ago?

    Not necessarily new infection. HRP2 stays in the blood after cure, so the test can stay positive for weeks. A film settles it.

Tell me about antimalarial drug resistance.

Mutations let some parasites survive a drug, and wide use of the drug selects them. Chloroquine resistance in P. falciparum, mainly from PfCRT mutations, now covers most of Africa.

  1. Where has artemisinin resistance been seen?

    First in South-East Asia, as slower clearance of the parasite. WHO has now confirmed partial resistance in Eritrea, Rwanda, Uganda and Tanzania.

Haematology and Immunology

Tell me about hypersplenism.

Hypersplenism is an enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the marrow works normally. It can occur with any cause of splenomegaly. As the spleen enlarges it can pool up to 40% of the red cell mass and 90% of the platelets. In tropical splenomegaly syndrome it causes severe anaemia, leucopenia and low platelets.

  1. Why are all three of his blood counts low?

    Hypersplenism. An enlarged spleen can pool up to 40% of the red cell mass and 90% of the platelets, so the counts fall while the marrow works normally.

  2. Splenectomy corrects his pancytopenia. Why is he still worse off without his spleen?

    The spleen also filters parasitised red cells. Without it, the risk of fulminant malaria rises.

Tell me how the red cell resists malaria.

Several inherited traits protect against malaria, and malaria has kept them common where it's endemic. Sickle cell trait protects against severe falciparum malaria. Women heterozygous for G6PD deficiency resist falciparum malaria. The thalassaemia carrier state gives some protection. Most West Africans lack the Duffy antigen, which P. vivax uses to enter the red cell.

  1. Why does Duffy negativity protect against vivax malaria?

    P. vivax uses the Duffy antigen to enter the red cell, and up to 98% of people of West African origin lack it.

  2. Does sickle cell disease protect against malaria the way the trait does?

    The protection is described for the carrier state. In sickle cell disease, repeated infarcts shrink the spleen, and malaria tends to be more severe without a working spleen.

Tell me about the anaemia of malaria.

Malaria destroys parasitised red cells, both in macrophages outside the vessels and by lysis inside them, most severely in falciparum. Chronic malaria adds an anaemia of chronic disorders, and hypersplenism pools red cells in a large spleen. Dyserythropoiesis, folate deficiency and malnutrition can add to it.

  1. Why is the serum iron low in chronic malaria?

    Inflammation raises hepcidin, which holds iron inside macrophages. The serum iron falls while ferritin stays normal or high.

  2. Can a blood transfusion pass on malaria?

    Yes. Malaria parasites stay alive in blood stored at 4°C.

Chemical Pathology

Tell me what you know about hypoglycaemia in malaria.

Malaria causes hypoglycaemia in two ways of its own: glucose is used up faster by anaerobic glycolysis, and gluconeogenesis in the liver is impaired. Quinine adds a third, by stimulating insulin release.

  1. How would you tell quinine-induced hypoglycaemia from hypoglycaemia caused by the malaria itself?

    By the plasma insulin, taken with the low glucose. Insulin is raised with quinine, and low when the malaria is the cause.

  2. Why is the plasma glucose taken into a fluoride tube?

    Fluoride stops blood cells using up glucose after the sample is taken, which would give a falsely low result.

Tell me what you know about lactic acidosis in falciparum malaria.

Parasitised red cells stick in small vessels and block them. Tissues short of oxygen switch to anaerobic glycolysis and make lactate. Bicarbonate falls and the anion gap widens: a high anion gap metabolic acidosis.

  1. What is the anion gap?

    Sodium plus potassium, minus chloride and bicarbonate. It stands for the anions that aren't measured, such as lactate.

  2. How does the patient compensate?

    By rapid, deep, laboured breathing, which blows off carbon dioxide.

  3. What else in malaria causes lactic acidosis?

    Severe anaemia, which carries too little oxygen to the tissues. Acidosis from anaemia alone has a better outlook.

Tell me what you know about blackwater fever.

Blackwater fever is an acute intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria, with haemoglobinuria, jaundice and acute kidney injury. It has been linked with quinine.

  1. Which results show that haemolysis is intravascular?

    Free haemoglobin in the plasma and urine, absent haptoglobin, methaemalbumin in the plasma, and haemosiderin in the urine.

  2. A child with P. malariae has swollen legs and heavy proteinuria. How is that kidney disease different from blackwater fever?

    P. malariae causes the nephrotic syndrome through immune complexes in the glomeruli, over months. Blackwater fever is acute intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria.

Tell me what you know about the serum proteins in tropical splenomegaly syndrome.

Years of malarial antigen drive many B-cell clones to make immunoglobulin, mostly IgM. The total protein rises with the globulins, and electrophoresis shows a broad polyclonal rise in the gamma region.

  1. How does a polyclonal rise differ from a paraprotein?

    A polyclonal rise is broad and diffuse, made by many clones. A paraprotein is a narrow, dense band made by one clone, as in myeloma or a B-cell lymphoma.

  2. How do you work out the globulin from routine results?

    Total protein minus albumin.

Pharmacology

Tell me what you know about artemisinin-based combination therapy.

An artemisinin given with a longer-acting partner drug, such as artemether with lumefantrine or artesunate with amodiaquine. The artemisinin kills blood-stage parasites very rapidly but is gone within hours. The partner clears the parasites left and protects the artemisinin against resistance, but once the artemisinin has gone the partner is left alone, so resistance to the partner can be selected.

  1. How do the artemisinins kill the parasite?

    They carry an endoperoxide bridge. Iron is thought to split the bridge, and the free radicals released kill the parasite.

  2. Which stages of the parasite do they act on?

    Blood-stage parasites of every human species, and young gametocytes but not mature ones. They have no effect on the liver stages.

  3. What harm can follow artesunate given for severe malaria?

    A delayed haemolysis, which typically starts two to three weeks after treatment and often needs a transfusion.

Tell me what you know about chloroquine.

A 4-aminoquinoline and a blood schizonticide. It builds up in the parasite's food vacuole and stops haem being crystallised into haemozoin, so free haem poisons the parasite. Resistance through PfCRT is now very common in P. falciparum. Itching is a common adverse effect in Africans.

  1. Why is chloroquine no longer used for most falciparum malaria?

    Mutations in PfCRT, a transporter in the parasite, make P. falciparum resistant, and resistance is now very common. Artemisinin-based combinations have replaced chloroquine for falciparum malaria in most endemic countries.

  2. Which related drug is still used in Africa, and with what?

    Amodiaquine, a 4-aminoquinoline closely related to chloroquine. It's given with artesunate as ASAQ, a first-line ACT in many African countries.

  3. Why isn't amodiaquine used for long-term prophylaxis?

    It can cause agranulocytosis, aplastic anaemia and liver injury. Serious toxicity is uncommon at treatment doses, but it seems to rise with long-term use.

Tell me what you know about quinine.

A quinoline from cinchona bark and a rapid blood schizonticide, though its mechanism is unknown. It treats severe malaria where intravenous artesunate isn't available. Its adverse effects include cinchonism, hypoglycaemia from insulin release and hypotension if infused too fast, and it's linked to blackwater fever.

  1. Why does quinine cause hypoglycaemia, and who is most at risk?

    It stimulates insulin release. Patients with severe malaria and pregnant women are most at risk.

  2. What is cinchonism?

    Tinnitus, headache, nausea, dizziness, flushing and visual disturbance, caused by quinine at treatment doses.

Tell me what you know about primaquine.

An 8-aminoquinoline that kills the liver forms of the parasite, including the dormant hypnozoites of P. vivax and P. ovale, and makes falciparum gametocytes non-infective. Its mechanism is unknown. It can cause haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia in G6PD deficiency.

  1. Why is G6PD tested before it's given?

    Primaquine can cause haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia in G6PD deficiency, so it's withheld until the patient's G6PD status is known.

  2. Why is it avoided in pregnancy?

    The fetus is relatively G6PD-deficient, so it's at risk of haemolysis.

Tell me how the antifolates act against malaria.

They block the parasite's folate pathway. Pyrimethamine and proguanil inhibit dihydrofolate reductase, and sulfadoxine inhibits dihydropteroate synthase, so sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine blocks two steps and acts synergistically. They act slowly on blood-stage parasites, and resistance comes from mutations in the two enzymes.

  1. Where is sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine used today?

    For intermittent preventive therapy in pregnancy after the first trimester, and with amodiaquine for seasonal chemoprevention in the Sahel. Resistance means it's no longer used alone to treat malaria.

  2. Why does long-term proguanil help in tropical splenomegaly syndrome?

    The syndrome is an abnormal immune response to repeated malaria. Daily proguanil suppresses the repeated infections, and the spleen shrinks; that response is one of the diagnostic criteria.

    Necrosis of a vessel wall where immune complexes or leaked plasma proteins are deposited, staining bright pink.
    Free radical
    A molecule with an unpaired electron, which reacts with and damages lipids, proteins and DNA.
    Functional asplenia (hyposplenism)
    A spleen that's present but can't do its work of filtering the blood and clearing bacteria. In sickle cell disease it starts in early childhood, while the spleen is still large.
    Gametocide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the sexual forms of the parasite, the gametocytes, so they can't pass the infection on to a mosquito.
    Gametocyte
    The male or female sexual form of Plasmodium in red cells. A biting mosquito takes gametocytes up, and the cycle goes on in the mosquito.
    Gangrene
    A clinical term for necrosis of a limb or part, usually from loss of blood supply.
    Granuloma
    A compact collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with multinucleate giant cells, ringed by lymphocytes.
    Haemoglobinopathy
    An inherited disorder caused by a mutation that changes the structure of a globin chain, such as sickle haemoglobin (Hb S) or Hb C.
    Haemosiderin
    A golden-brown pigment of stored iron, seen where red cells have broken down or iron is in excess.
    Haemozoin (malarial pigment)
    A brown-black pigment the parasite leaves from the haemoglobin it digests. Ruptured red cells release it, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.
    Haptoglobin
    A plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The pair is removed from the circulation, so the haptoglobin level falls in haemolysis.
    Haemoglobin electrophoresis
    A test that moves haemoglobins along a strip in an electric field, so each kind shows as a band at its own position.
    Hepcidin
    A hormone that keeps iron inside macrophages and blocks its absorption from the gut. Inflammation raises it, which starves the marrow of iron.
    Howell-Jolly body
    A small remnant of DNA inside a red cell. Howell-Jolly bodies show on the blood film when the spleen has stopped working.
    High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
    A test that passes haemoglobin through a column. Each kind leaves at its own time, so the machine names it and measures its share of the total.
    HRP2 (histidine-rich protein 2)
    A protein made by P. falciparum and no other species, and the antigen most malaria rapid diagnostic tests detect.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in a tissue, in cells that can divide.
    Hypersplenism
    An enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the bone marrow works normally.
    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without more cells.
    Hypnozoite
    A dormant liver form of P. vivax and P. ovale. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse.
    Hypoxia
    Too little oxygen reaching the cells.
    Infarct
    An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by loss of the blood supply, in sickle cell disease usually from vessels blocked by sickled cells.
    Intermittent preventive therapy
    Treatment doses of an antimalarial given at set times to people at high risk, such as pregnant women, whether or not they're infected.
    Iron chelation
    Treatment with a drug that binds iron tightly so it can leave the body in urine or faeces. It treats the iron overload of repeated blood transfusion.
    Irreversibly sickled cell
    A red cell whose membrane has been damaged by many cycles of sickling, so it stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen.
    Ischaemia
    Too little blood reaching a tissue: less oxygen, and less of everything else the blood brings and takes away.
    Karyolysis
    A nucleus fading away as its DNA is broken down.
    Karyorrhexis
    A pyknotic nucleus breaking into fragments.
    Lactic acidosis
    A high anion gap metabolic acidosis from lactate building up in the blood. Its commonest cause is tissue short of oxygen, from poor perfusion, hypoxia or severe anaemia.
    Langhans giant cell
    A multinucleate cell formed when macrophages fuse. Its nuclei sit in a horseshoe around the edge of the cell.
    Lipofuscin
    A yellow-brown 'wear and tear' pigment of oxidised lipid, building up in long-lived cells with age.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Necrosis in which enzymes digest the dead tissue into a liquid.
    Merozoite
    The form of Plasmodium released when an infected liver cell or red cell bursts. Each merozoite invades a red cell.
    Metaplasia
    The replacement of one mature cell type by another, better able to stand a particular stress.
    Metastatic calcification
    Calcium deposited in normal tissue because the blood calcium is high.
    Methaemoglobinaemia
    Haemoglobin in the blood with its iron oxidised from the Fe2+ to the Fe3+ state. Drugs such as primaquine can cause it, and the patient looks cyanosed.
    Necrosis
    Death of cells in a living tissue, with their membranes breaking and their contents leaking out, which brings in inflammation.
    Necrotising granuloma
    A granuloma whose centre has died. In tuberculosis the dead centre is caseous.
    Opsonisation
    Coating of a microbe with antibody or complement so phagocytes can recognise and ingest it.
    Osteomyelitis
    Infection of bone and its marrow. In sickle cell disease bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarction.
    P-selectin
    An adhesion molecule that helps sickle cells stick to the walls of blood vessels, one step in vaso-occlusion.
    Pancytopenia
    A fall in all three blood cell lines: red cells, white cells and platelets.
    Paraprotein
    One immunoglobulin made in excess by a single clone of B cells. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a narrow, dense band, usually in the gamma region.
    Parasitaemia
    Malaria parasites in the circulating blood, counted on a film as the share of red cells infected or the number per microlitre.
    Penicillin-binding protein (PBP)
    A bacterial enzyme that cross-links the peptide chains of peptidoglycan, which gives the cell wall its strength. Beta-lactam antibiotics bind its active site and block it.
    Pigment gallstone
    A gallstone made of calcium bilirubinate. It forms when bile carries a high load of unconjugated bilirubin, as in chronic haemolysis.
    pLDH (Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase)
    An enzyme of the malaria parasite, detected by some rapid diagnostic tests. pLDH tests turn negative within days of cure.
    Polyclonal response
    Immunoglobulins made by many B-cell clones at once, as in chronic infection. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a broad, diffuse rise in the gamma region.
    Polymerisation of Hb S
    Deoxygenated sickle haemoglobin molecules binding one another into long fibres. The fibres distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
    Pyknosis
    A nucleus that has shrunk and stains dark, as its chromatin condenses.
    Radical cure
    Treatment that clears malaria parasites from both the blood and the liver, so P. vivax or P. ovale can't relapse. It needs a blood-stage drug plus primaquine or tafenoquine.
    Recrudescence
    The return of malaria from blood-stage parasites that treatment didn't clear. A relapse is different: it comes from dormant liver forms.
    Ischaemia–reperfusion injury
    Further injury to ischaemic tissue when its blood supply comes back.
    Reticulocyte
    A young red cell, just out of the marrow, that still holds RNA. The reticulocyte count shows how well the marrow is making red cells.
    Ribonucleotide reductase
    The enzyme that turns ribonucleotides into the deoxyribonucleotides a cell needs to copy its DNA. Blocking it stops dividing cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.
    Ring haemorrhage
    A small haemorrhage in the brain's white matter arranged as a ring around a small vessel, seen at autopsy in cerebral malaria.
    Schizont
    A Plasmodium form whose nucleus has divided many times. It breaks up into merozoites when the liver cell or red cell bursts.
    Schüffner's dots
    Fine dots in a red cell infected with P. vivax or P. ovale, seen on a well-stained Giemsa film as the parasite matures.
    Senescence
    A permanent stop to cell division that cells reach with age.
    Sequestration
    Red cells carrying maturing P. falciparum sticking to the endothelium of small vessels, through the parasite protein PfEMP1, so they leave the circulating blood and block the capillaries.
    Siderofibrotic nodule
    A small nodule of fibrous tissue holding iron pigment and calcium, found in the scarred spleen of sickle cell disease beside vessels full of sickled cells.
    Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis
    Lymphocytes filling the sinusoids of the liver on biopsy. It supports a diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome but isn't specific to it.
    Pitting
    The spleen's removal of inclusions, such as Howell-Jolly bodies and Heinz bodies, from red cells it then lets go back into the blood.
    Splenic sequestration
    Sudden pooling of sickled red cells in the spleen, which enlarges while the haemoglobin falls. Seen mostly in infants with sickle cell disease.
    Splenomegaly
    Enlargement of the spleen. A massive spleen can reach the right iliac fossa.
    Sporozoite
    The form of Plasmodium that an infected female Anopheles mosquito injects when it bites. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver.
    Steatosis (fatty change)
    Triglyceride building up inside cells, seen most in the liver.
    Tissue schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the parasites developing or lying dormant in the liver, such as primaquine.
    Trophozoite
    The growing stage of Plasmodium inside a red cell. On a stained film the young trophozoite looks like a ring.
    Unconjugated bilirubin
    Bilirubin that the liver hasn't yet joined to glucuronate. It isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin and doesn't pass into the urine.
    Urobilinogen
    A colourless product that gut bacteria make from bilirubin. Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver, and a little passes in the urine.
    Vaso-occlusion
    Blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red cells, which causes ischaemia, pain and infarction in the tissue they supply.
    Villous lymphocyte
    A lymphocyte with fine hair-like projections from its cytoplasm. Villous lymphocytes in the blood suggest splenic marginal zone lymphoma.
